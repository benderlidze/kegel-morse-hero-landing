# What Do Kegels Actually Do for Men?

> **Quick answer:** Kegels train the pelvic floor muscles that help close the urethra and anus, support the pelvic organs, and respond when pressure rises during coughing, lifting, or exercise. They may improve some bladder or bowel-control problems and are often used around prostate treatment. They are not a universal treatment for erection, ejaculation, pain, or every urinary symptom.

## What a Kegel Trains

A Kegel is a deliberate contraction and full release of the pelvic floor. For a man, the correct sensation is a small squeeze around the anus and urethra with an inward and upward lift. The penis may retract slightly and the scrotum may lift.

Training develops three abilities: generating force, holding a submaximal contraction, and reacting quickly. Relaxation matters just as much. A muscle that never releases is not functioning normally, even if it feels “strong.”

## What Kegels May Help

Pelvic floor muscle training is commonly used for urinary leakage, particularly after prostate surgery. It can also support bowel control. Some men notice better awareness and coordination before they notice more strength.

Sexual outcomes are less predictable. Pelvic floor muscles participate in erection and ejaculation, but erectile dysfunction and ejaculation problems can have vascular, neurological, hormonal, medication-related, psychological, or pelvic-pain causes. Kegels may be one part of care, not a guaranteed fix.

## What Kegels Do Not Do

Kegels do not enlarge the penis, diagnose pelvic floor dysfunction, or correct every urinary symptom. They are also not a substitute for assessment when there is pain, blood in urine, difficulty starting urine, recurrent infection, a new lump, or sudden change.

More squeezing is not always better. If the pelvic floor is overactive or cannot relax, repeated strengthening can aggravate pelvic, urinary, bowel, or sexual symptoms. Read [Can You Do Too Many Kegels?](/articles/can-you-do-too-many-kegels/) for warning signs.

## A Safe Starting Check

1. Sit or lie comfortably and keep breathing.
2. Gently close around the anus as if preventing gas.
3. Add the feeling of stopping urine, without actually practising during urination.
4. Feel a lift rather than a push downward.
5. Release completely before the next repetition.

Start below your maximum. Quality, breathing, and a clear release matter more than a large repetition count. If you cannot identify the movement, use the methods in [How to Find Your Pelvic Floor Muscles](/articles/how-to-find-pelvic-floor-muscles-men/).

## When to Get Help

A pelvic health physiotherapist can assess strength, endurance, quick response, relaxation, breathing, and pressure management. Seek medical advice for persistent leakage, pain, difficulty emptying the bladder or bowel, or symptoms that worsen with exercise.
