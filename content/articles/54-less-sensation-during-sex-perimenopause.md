# Less Sensation During Sex in Perimenopause: What Helps

Less feeling during sex and perimenopause often go together. As estrogen falls, vaginal and vulval tissue can become thinner, drier and less well supplied with blood, and arousal can take longer to build. Treatments a doctor can discuss, such as vaginal estrogen, plus lubricants, moisturisers and pelvic floor training, help many women feel more again.

> **Short answer:** Reduced sensation in perimenopause is usually linked to lower estrogen (genitourinary syndrome of menopause), reduced blood flow and sometimes a weak or tense pelvic floor. It is treatable, and it tends not to improve on its own, so it is worth raising with a doctor.

## Less Feeling During Sex and Perimenopause: What's the Link?

Perimenopause is the transition before periods stop, often lasting several years. The NHS lists vaginal dryness, discomfort, painful sex and reduced sex drive among symptoms of both perimenopause and menopause. Many women notice these changes before their periods have actually ended, which is why they can be confusing.

### Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)

GSM is the medical name for the changes lower estrogen causes in the vulva, vagina and bladder. Clinical references describe:

- Thinner, less elastic vaginal lining.
- Reduced natural lubrication, burning or irritation.
- Changes to the clitoris and vaginal opening.
- Reduced blood flow and slower nerve signals in the area.
- Urinary urgency, frequency or repeated infections.

Each of these can dull sensation directly, or make sex uncomfortable enough that it is hard to relax into pleasure. Unlike hot flushes, GSM symptoms are usually chronic and rarely improve without treatment.

### Blood flow and arousal

Genital sensation depends on blood flow. During arousal, the clitoris and vaginal tissue swell and become more sensitive. With lower estrogen, that response can be slower and weaker, so you may need more time and more direct stimulation to feel the same.

### The pelvic floor

The pelvic floor muscles surround the vagina and contract during orgasm. Weak muscles can mean weaker sensations and orgasms; muscles that are too tight, often after painful sex, can cause pain and make pleasure harder to reach. Both patterns are common in midlife, and they need different approaches. ### Other factors

Sleep disruption, stress, mood changes, relationship strain, and medicines such as some antidepressants can all reduce desire and sensation. Conditions like diabetes can also affect nerves and blood flow.

## Treatments a Doctor Can Discuss

| Option | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lubricant | Reduces friction during sex | Water- or silicone-based; avoid perfumed products |
| Vaginal moisturiser | Rehydrates tissue over time | Used regularly, often 2–3 times a week, not just before sex |
| Low-dose vaginal estrogen | Restores tissue thickness, moisture and blood flow locally | Prescription; cream, tablet, pessary or ring |
| Vaginal DHEA (prasterone) or ospemifene | Alternatives for persistent symptoms | Prescription; suitability depends on your history |
| Systemic HRT | Treats wider perimenopause symptoms | Prescription; discuss benefits and risks |
| Pelvic floor physiotherapy | Strengthens or relaxes the muscles as needed | Especially if you also leak or have pain |

Clinical guidance suggests starting with moisturisers and lubricants for mild symptoms, and considering low-dose vaginal estrogen or other prescription options when symptoms persist. Because GSM is ongoing, symptoms often return if treatment stops. If you have had breast cancer or another hormone-sensitive condition, talk to your specialist before using any hormonal product.

## Can Pelvic Floor Training Help?

It may help some women, as part of a wider plan. Pelvic floor exercises support bladder control, which often weakens in midlife, and stronger, better-coordinated muscles may contribute to arousal and orgasm. Research specifically in perimenopause is limited, so treat it as a useful addition, not a replacement for treating dryness and tissue changes.

A simple starting routine:

1. Sit or lie comfortably and breathe out.
2. Gently close and lift around the vagina and anus, as if stopping wind and urine.
3. Hold for 3–5 seconds, then release completely for the same time.
4. Repeat 8–10 times, then do a few quick 1-second squeezes.
5. Practise once or twice a day and build slowly over weeks.

If squeezing hurts or sex is painful, start with relaxation instead and see a pelvic health physiotherapist. ## When to See a Doctor

Book an appointment if:

- Sex is painful, or dryness has lasted a few weeks despite self-care.
- You have bleeding after sex, between periods or after menopause.
- You notice unusual discharge, itching or burning.
- You feel heaviness, dragging or a bulge in the vagina.
- You leak urine or have repeated urine infections.
- Numbness is getting worse, or came on suddenly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do I have less sensation during sex in perimenopause?

Falling estrogen can thin and dry vaginal tissue and reduce blood flow, so arousal is slower and sensation weaker. Pelvic floor changes, stress, sleep and some medicines can add to it.

### Does vaginal estrogen help with sensation?

For many women it improves tissue thickness, moisture and comfort, which can make sex more pleasurable. Ask a doctor whether it suits you.

### Can Kegels increase sensitivity in perimenopause?

They may help arousal and orgasm for some women, but they do not fix dryness or thinning tissue. They work best alongside treatment for GSM.

### Will it get better on its own?

GSM symptoms usually do not improve without treatment and may slowly worsen, so it is worth getting help early.

*This article is for general education and is not medical advice. For painful sex, bleeding or ongoing symptoms, see your doctor, a menopause specialist or a pelvic floor physical therapist.*
