# The Knack for Women: Before You Cough, Sneeze or Lift

> **Quick answer:** The Knack is a quick, deliberate pelvic floor contraction immediately before and during a cough, sneeze, laugh, lift, or other pressure spike. It is a timing skill, not an all-day clench, and it works best when followed by a full release.

## Why Timing Matters

Coughing, sneezing, jumping, and lifting rapidly increase pressure inside the abdomen. A well-timed pelvic floor lift helps support the urethra before that pressure arrives. Contracting after the cough is too late for that event.

## How to Practise the Knack

1. Sit or stand comfortably and breathe normally.
2. Make a quick inward and upward pelvic floor lift.
3. Give one gentle cough while holding the lift.
4. Release fully after the cough.
5. Rest before repeating.

Once the timing is clear, use it before a real sneeze, laugh, transition from sitting to standing, or manageable household lift. Keep the effort proportional; you do not need a maximum squeeze for every task.

## Common Mistakes

- Waiting until after the cough.
- Holding the breath and bracing the whole abdomen.
- Squeezing the buttocks and thighs instead of lifting internally.
- Pushing downward.
- Staying clenched after the task.
- Using the technique to mask persistent symptoms without assessment.

## The Knack Is Not a Full Treatment Plan

It may reduce some stress-leakage events, but recurring leakage can need a broader programme of strength, endurance, bladder habits, pressure management, and professional assessment. A vaginal bulge, pain, urgency, or difficulty emptying may indicate a different or additional problem.

NICE recommends at least three months of supervised pelvic floor muscle training for women with stress or mixed urinary incontinence. The Knack can be one functional skill within that programme.

## When to Seek Help

See a clinician or pelvic health physiotherapist if leakage is frequent, worsening, accompanied by pain or a bulge, or not improving with a consistent programme. Burning, blood, fever, or sudden urinary changes need medical evaluation.

*This article is for general education and is not medical advice. Persistent urinary leakage deserves individualized assessment.*
