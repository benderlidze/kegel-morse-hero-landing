# How Often Should Men Do Kegels: Daily or Every Other Day?

> **Quick answer:** Most NHS pelvic floor programs for men use daily practice, often one to three planned sessions. Some clinical handouts prescribe more. Every-other-day training can be a sensible temporary reduction if a beginner cannot maintain correct technique or develops fatigue, but pain or worsening urinary symptoms are reasons to stop and get assessed—not simply to add a rest day and push on.

There is no universal schedule for every man because “Kegels” may refer to rehabilitation after prostate treatment, leakage training, general strengthening, or an exercise someone started without an assessment.

Frequency matters less than using the correct muscles, relaxing fully, and following a program that matches the problem.

## Daily vs Every Other Day

| Schedule | When it may fit | Main risk |
|---|---|---|
| Daily, 1 quality session | A cautious beginner building coordination | Progress may be slower but technique is easier to monitor |
| Daily, 2–3 sessions | A common clinical structure for weakness or leakage | Fatigue if contractions are too strong or rests are rushed |
| Daily, more frequent sessions | Sometimes used in specific clinical handouts | Easy to turn into constant clenching without guidance |
| Every other day | Temporary option when learning or after mild fatigue | May not match a postoperative or clinician-prescribed plan |

Royal Free London notes that there is no fixed advice that suits everyone and gives three to six daily sets as one possible approach. Dynamic Health NHS advises aiming for three times a day, while also stressing full relaxation and gradual progression.

Those differences are a useful reminder: a handout is a starting framework, not a personalized prescription.

## Should Men Do Kegels Every Day?

Usually, yes—when strengthening is appropriate and the contractions are comfortable.

Pelvic floor rehabilitation commonly uses short daily practice because learning coordination requires repetition. Daily does not mean squeezing whenever you remember. It means one planned routine, followed by complete rest between contractions and between sessions.

One controlled session is better than three rushed sessions in which the abdomen, thighs, or buttocks take over.

## When Every Other Day May Be Better

Reducing frequency can help when:

- You are new and cannot keep surrounding muscles relaxed.
- The final repetitions are clearly weaker or less coordinated.
- You feel mild muscular fatigue that resolves with rest and is not pelvic pain.
- A physiotherapist has asked you to space sessions differently.

Use the easier day to practice relaxed breathing and awareness, not to clench throughout normal activities.

If discomfort returns each time you restart, do not keep experimenting with volume. Get assessed.

## A Conservative Starting Schedule

For a man without pain who has not been given a medical program, a cautious technique-first start could look like this:

1. Begin with one session a day.
2. Perform up to five comfortable contractions, holding only as long as you can breathe normally.
3. Rest at least as long as each contraction.
4. Add a few quick contractions only if you can release fully afterward.
5. After one to two weeks of clean technique, consider a second daily session.
6. Progress toward a clinician-recommended target rather than chasing the highest count.

This is a conservative example, not a treatment plan. After prostate surgery, radiotherapy, or treatment for incontinence, follow the schedule and start date given by your clinical team.

## Signs Your Current Frequency Is Too High

Reduce or stop the routine if you notice:

- Contractions becoming weaker within the set.
- Your abdomen, buttocks, or thighs taking over.
- Breath-holding or straining.
- An inability to feel the muscles release.
- New pelvic, perineal, lower abdominal, or testicular pain.
- Increased urinary urgency or frequency.
- Difficulty starting urine or emptying the bladder.
- Pain with erection or ejaculation.

Fatigue can mean the dose is too high. Pain and urinary changes can also point to poor technique, an overactive pelvic floor, or a different condition that needs evaluation.

## How Long Until Frequency Produces Results?

Pelvic floor training is usually measured in weeks and months, not days. Dynamic Health NHS says coordination may start to improve after two to three weeks, while muscle strengthening can take four to six months.

Do not increase frequency just because nothing dramatic happened in the first week. Track a relevant outcome—such as leakage episodes, pad use, or ability to complete the prescribed holds—rather than judging progress by soreness.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it okay to do Kegels every day?

Yes, daily practice is common in clinical programs when strengthening is appropriate. The correct number of sessions depends on the condition, technique, and instructions from your clinician.

### Do pelvic floor muscles need rest days?

They need full relaxation between repetitions and sessions. Some people also benefit from a lower-frequency schedule while learning. A prescribed rehabilitation plan may still use daily sessions.

### Can I do Kegels throughout the day?

Avoid continuous or random squeezing. Planned sessions make it easier to control total volume and notice whether you fully relax.

### What if Kegels hurt the next day?

Stop the provoking routine. Pelvic floor exercises should not cause pain. Persistent or recurring pain should be assessed by a healthcare professional or pelvic floor physiotherapist.
