How to Do Kegels Without Doing Them Wrong

Reviewed September 28, 2026By Kegel Morse HeroEvidence-aware guide

You squeeze something, count to three, and hope it is the right muscle. Sound familiar? The tricky part of Kegels is that a lot of effort can feel convincing even when you are mostly clenching your butt or bracing your stomach.

This guide walks men through a simple technique check. Start with one controlled repetition before worrying about a daily total.

First, Check That Strengthening Fits

If you already have pelvic pain, difficulty emptying your bladder, or symptoms that get worse with squeezing, get assessed before starting a strengthening routine.

A pelvic floor can have trouble relaxing as well as contracting. Cleveland Clinic's guide to hypertonic pelvic floor explains why some people need treatment focused on restoring relaxation and coordination.

Step 1: Find the Lift

Lie on your back with your knees bent, or sit comfortably with your feet supported. Let your thighs and buttocks relax.

Imagine stopping yourself from passing gas. Gently close around the back passage and draw the muscles inward and upward. Avoid pushing down as though trying to empty your bowels.

In men, the base of the penis may move slightly toward the abdomen during a contraction. This is a possible cue, not a pass-or-fail test. University Hospitals Sussex describes the movement.

If you are still unsure what you are feeling, see How to Find Your Pelvic Floor Muscles.

Step 2: Do One Complete Repetition

  1. Start in a comfortable position, with no deliberate squeeze.
  2. Lift the pelvic floor inward.
  3. Hold for about three seconds while breathing normally.
  4. Let the contraction go completely.
  5. Rest for about three seconds, or longer if you need time to release.

This brief hold-and-rest pattern follows Mayo Clinic's beginner guidance. If you cannot maintain the hold without straining, shorten it. The goal is a controlled repetition, not winning a countdown.

Step 3: Check What the Rest of Your Body Is Doing

What you noticeWhat to try
You stop breathingPause, return to easy breathing, and retry with less effort.
Your buttocks clench or thighs squeeze togetherReset your position and focus on the small internal lift.
You brace your belly hardReduce the effort; let your abdomen move naturally with breathing.
You push downwardStop and return to the inward-lift cue. Get help if the direction remains unclear.
You cannot feel yourself releaseRest longer instead of immediately adding another squeeze.

These checks can highlight obvious mistakes, but they cannot confirm your technique as reliably as a professional assessment.

Step 4: Build the Routine After the Movement

Try a few comfortable repetitions first. Mayo Clinic describes gradually working toward 10 to 15 repetitions per set, with three sets a day. That is general guidance, not a target everyone must reach immediately.

Your clinician may recommend a different hold, rest, or frequency. NIDDK advises an individual exercise plan and cautions against overdoing it.

As control improves, practice sitting and standing too. Keep the same attention on breathing and release.

Keep Toilet Time Separate

Do not make stopping and restarting your urine stream a regular exercise. It can interfere with bladder emptying. The imagined “hold in gas” cue lets you practice away from the toilet.

And do not keep a background squeeze going all day. Between repetitions and sessions, let the muscles rest.

Still Unsure? Get Feedback

If you cannot identify a clear lift and release, a pelvic floor physiotherapist can check what you are doing. Biofeedback may help you understand the contraction and relaxation.

Stop exercises that cause pain or worsening symptoms. For further reading, see Am I Doing Kegels Wrong? and Reverse Kegels for Men.
