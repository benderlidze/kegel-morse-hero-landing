What Do Kegels Do for Men Sexually? Benefits in Bed

Reviewed November 23, 2026By Sergei ProstokishynEvidence-aware guide

Sexually, Kegels train the muscles at the base of the penis that help keep blood in an erection and that contract rhythmically during ejaculation. Studies show pelvic floor training may help some men with erectile dysfunction and with premature ejaculation, mostly in small, supervised programs. Outside the bedroom, the best evidence is for bladder control, after-dribble and recovery after prostate surgery.

Short answer: Kegels may improve erection firmness, ejaculatory control and bladder control for some men, usually over weeks to months. They are not a guaranteed fix, and they do not make the penis bigger.

What a Kegel Actually Trains

A Kegel is a deliberate squeeze and full release of the pelvic floor, the sling of muscle between the pubic bone and the tailbone. It should feel like a small inward lift around the anus and the base of the penis, as if stopping gas and urine at once.

Two muscles matter most for sex:

  • Ischiocavernosus: compresses the base of the erect penis and helps maintain rigidity.
  • Bulbospongiosus (bulbocavernosus): helps with rigidity, contracts rhythmically during ejaculation, and helps clear the urethra after urination.

Training builds force, endurance and quick on-off control. Relaxation matters as much as squeezing. If you are not sure you are using the right muscles, start with How to Find Your Pelvic Floor Muscles.

What Do Kegels Do for Men Sexually?

Here is what the research supports, roughly from strongest to weakest.

Erections

The best-known trial randomized 55 men with erectile dysfunction. Pelvic floor exercises with biofeedback and lifestyle advice improved erectile function more than lifestyle advice alone at three months. That is promising, but it was a small study, it included professional feedback, and not every man improved.

Newer evidence is mixed. A 2024 meta-analysis found clear benefit from aerobic exercise for ED but no significant improvement in its pelvic floor training subgroup. The fair summary: Kegels may help some men, especially when weak or poorly coordinated muscles are part of the problem, but ED is usually a blood vessel, nerve, hormone, medication or stress issue first. Read Can Kegels Help Erectile Dysfunction? for the full evidence.

Ejaculation control and lasting longer

In a 2014 study by Pastore and colleagues, 33 of 40 men with lifelong premature ejaculation gained control over the ejaculatory reflex after 12 weeks of pelvic floor rehabilitation. The program included biofeedback and electrical stimulation, not just home squeezes, and there was no large control group. A 2019 systematic review called pelvic floor training promising for both ED and PE but could not identify one best protocol.

The likely mechanism is awareness and control: learning to notice tension rising and to relax or time a contraction on purpose. More detail is in Do Kegels Help Premature Ejaculation? and the practical Kegel Exercises to Last Longer in Bed.

Orgasm, sensation and libido

Some men report stronger orgasms after training. That is plausible, since these muscles produce the contractions of orgasm, but it has not been well measured. Kegels are also not proven to raise penile sensitivity, testosterone or sex drive. Loss of feeling can have medical causes such as diabetes, some medicines or low testosterone, so it deserves a clinician's look; see How to Increase Male Sensitivity.

After-dribble, the few drops that escape after you zip up, happens because urine stays in the urethra. A firm Kegel straight after urinating, or gentle pressure behind the scrotum, can help clear it, as described by NHS continence services. Before and after prostate surgery, a 2024 review of nine trials found better continence recovery at one, three and six months with exercise, though the difference was no longer significant at 12 months. If you are in that situation, follow your surgical team's plan.

What Kegels Do Not Do

  • They do not enlarge the penis, in length or girth.
  • They do not treat ED caused by heart disease, diabetes or medication.
  • They do not replace assessment for pain, blood in urine, a weak stream or trouble starting.

More squeezing is not always better. If the pelvic floor is already tight or overactive, extra Kegels can worsen pelvic pain, urinary hesitancy, painful ejaculation or even early ejaculation. In that case relaxation work such as reverse Kegels is the better first step, ideally with a pelvic floor physical therapist.

A Simple 6-Week Starting Plan

  1. Week 1: Find the muscle. Lie down, breathe normally, squeeze and lift for 2–3 seconds, then fully release for the same time. 8–10 reps, twice a day.
  2. Weeks 2–3: Build holds to 5 seconds with 5 seconds of rest. Add a few quick 1-second squeezes at the end of each set.
  3. Weeks 4–6: Practice sitting and standing. Work toward 8–10 second holds if they stay comfortable, plus a set of quick flicks.
  4. Every session: Release fully between reps, keep your buttocks, thighs and abs quiet, and never practice while urinating.
  5. Stop and get advice if you feel pain, a constant gripping sensation or worse urinary symptoms.

How Long Until You Notice Results?

Most study programs ran about 12 weeks. Men usually notice better awareness and control first, then firmer contractions, and only later any sexual change. No difference at all often means the main cause lies elsewhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the kegel exercise benefits for men in bed?

They may help firmer erections, better ejaculatory control and a stronger feeling of control during sex for some men. Evidence comes from small studies, so expect a possible improvement, not a guarantee.

What are realistic kegel exercises for men results?

Studies usually measured results after about 12 weeks. Bladder control and after-dribble tend to respond most reliably; sexual results vary much more from man to man.

Do Kegels make you last longer?

They may help some men with premature ejaculation, mainly through better awareness and control. They work best alongside techniques such as stop-start, and with a clinician if PE is lifelong or causing distress. This article is for general education and is not medical advice. For erection, ejaculation, urinary or pelvic pain problems, see a urologist, sexual medicine clinician or pelvic floor physical therapist.
