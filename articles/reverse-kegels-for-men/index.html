Reverse Kegels for Men: The Skill You Miss When You Only Squeeze

Reviewed September 28, 2026By Kegel Morse HeroEvidence-aware guide

You know how to squeeze. But can you actually let go afterward? If every pelvic floor session is about holding harder, the release can become an afterthought.

Reverse Kegels are a common name for deliberately relaxing and lengthening the pelvic floor. They are not a competition to push downward. For some men, learning that distinction is more useful than adding another set.

What a Reverse Kegel Actually Means

A regular Kegel involves a lift and squeeze. Relaxation lets the muscles return from that contraction. The aim is comfortable movement, not a dramatic sensation or visible motion.

Your breathing provides a useful cue: during an easy inhale, the diaphragm descends and the pelvic floor normally lengthens. Think of allowing that movement rather than forcing it. Plymouth's NHS relaxation guide describes this connection.

Tight Does Not Automatically Mean Strong

A pelvic floor that stays contracted may struggle to coordinate everyday functions. Pelvic discomfort, difficulty starting urine, constipation, or pain with ejaculation can occur with an overactive pelvic floor. They can also have other causes.

That list is a reason to get assessed, not a home diagnostic test. Cleveland Clinic describes physical therapy as a central part of treatment for diagnosed hypertonicity.

Try a Gentle Release

  1. Lie comfortably with knees bent and your head supported.
  2. Let your jaw, belly, and buttocks soften.
  3. Inhale slowly through your nose, allowing your abdomen and lower ribs to expand.
  4. Imagine the area underneath your pelvis becoming softer and less gripped.
  5. Exhale easily. Let the breath return without adding a deliberate squeeze.

Try a few comfortable breaths. Do not hold your breath, bear down, or strain as though pushing out stool. Stop if this produces pain or worsens symptoms.

When More Squeezing Is the Wrong Next Step

If exercise brings on pain or leaves you feeling persistently clenched, pause the provoking routine and ask a pelvic health physiotherapist for help. They can assess both contraction and release.

The 2025 AUA guideline recap discusses specialist physiotherapy for men with pelvic muscle pain, including approaches that reduce resting muscle tension. This is tailored care for assessed patients, not evidence that every man needs reverse Kegels.

Should You Stop Regular Kegels?

That depends on why you are training. Some people need strengthening; others need relaxation or a combination. A clinician can help decide when symptoms are present.
