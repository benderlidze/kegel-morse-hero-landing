Lower Belly Pain After Kegels: Why It Happens

Reviewed October 28, 2026By Kegel Morse HeroEvidence-aware guide

Quick answer: Lower belly pain after Kegels is not a sign that the exercise is working. Common exercise-related explanations include bracing the abdominal muscles, holding your breath, straining downward, doing too much, or tightening a pelvic floor that already has trouble relaxing. Abdominal pain also has many unrelated causes, so recurring, worsening, or severe pain needs medical assessment.

Stop the session that provoked the pain. Do not test it with a harder squeeze.

Why Kegels Can Make the Lower Abdomen Hurt

You are bracing your abs instead of isolating the pelvic floor

A correct pelvic floor contraction is a small inward and upward lift. If your stomach visibly hardens or pulls in, you may be substituting the abdominal wall for the target muscles.

Repeated hard bracing can leave the lower abdomen sore. Royal Free London advises keeping the stomach, thighs, and buttocks relaxed and breathing normally during pelvic floor contractions.

You are holding your breath

Breath-holding increases pressure through the abdomen. It often happens when a contraction is too strong or a hold lasts longer than you can control.

Use an intensity that lets you speak or breathe normally. End the repetition as soon as you need to brace.

You are pushing down instead of lifting

Some people bear down as if emptying the bowel. That is not a strengthening contraction and can increase pressure in the lower abdomen and pelvis.

The correct cue is “close and lift,” followed by a complete release—not a forceful push.

The volume is too high

Long maximum-effort holds, hundreds of repetitions, or random squeezing throughout the day can fatigue the pelvic floor and nearby muscles.

More volume is not automatically more effective. The quality of the contraction and relaxation matters more than reaching a large number.

Your pelvic floor may already be overactive

A hypertonic pelvic floor stays contracted or has difficulty relaxing. Cleveland Clinic lists pelvic, lower-back, or hip pain along with urinary, bowel, and sexual symptoms among possible features.

That does not mean lower abdominal pain proves hypertonicity. It means adding strengthening without an assessment can be the wrong response when pain, urinary hesitancy, constipation, or pain with ejaculation are also present.

The pain may not be caused by Kegels

Abdominal pain can come from the bowel, urinary tract, abdominal wall, a hernia, kidney stones, appendicitis, infection, or other conditions. The timing after exercise does not prove the exercise is the cause.

What to Do Now

  1. Stop the exercise that caused the pain.
  2. Let the abdomen, buttocks, thighs, and pelvic floor relax.
  3. Breathe comfortably without forcing the belly outward or pulling it inward.
  4. Note where the pain is, how long it lasts, and whether urination, bowel movements, lifting, coughing, or eating changes it.
  5. If it settles completely and there are no warning signs, restart only with a gentler technique after a rest period.
  6. If it returns, do not keep adjusting the routine alone; arrange an assessment.

A pelvic floor physiotherapist can examine contraction, relaxation, breathing, abdominal substitution, and pressure management. A primary-care clinician or urologist can assess non-muscular causes.

A Technique Check Before You Restart

Try this only when you are pain-free:

  • Lie down with knees bent.
  • Place one hand lightly on the lower abdomen.
  • Exhale normally and make a gentle pelvic floor lift, as if preventing gas.
  • Keep the abdomen mostly soft; do not pull the navel hard toward the spine.
  • Hold for only two or three comfortable seconds.
  • Release completely for at least the same amount of time.
  • Stop immediately if pain returns.

If you cannot feel a small lift without bracing, professional feedback is more useful than adding repetitions.

When to Seek Medical Help

Arrange a routine medical appointment if the pain keeps returning, does not go away, or comes with a lump or bulge in the abdomen or groin.

Seek urgent medical advice if abdominal pain suddenly becomes much worse or occurs with persistent vomiting, blood in stool or urine, fever, painful urination, or significant testicular pain or swelling.

Seek emergency care for sudden severe abdominal pain, inability to urinate, inability to pass stool or gas, vomiting blood, black stool, breathing difficulty, fainting, or rapidly worsening symptoms.

Follow your surgeon’s instructions if the pain began during recovery from prostate, abdominal, or pelvic surgery.

When Should You Switch to Reverse Kegels?

Do not assume pain means you should force the opposite movement. A reverse Kegel is a gentle release, not bearing down.

Relaxed diaphragmatic breathing may be comfortable, but persistent pain still needs evaluation. Read Reverse Kegels for Men for the difference between releasing and straining.

For signs of overtraining, see Can You Do Too Many Kegels?.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is lower abdominal soreness normal after Kegels?

Kegels should not produce lower abdominal pain. Mild soreness may come from accidentally bracing other muscles, but recurring discomfort is a reason to stop and review the technique.

Can Kegels cause a hernia?

Lower belly or groin pain and a bulge need medical assessment. Do not assume Kegels caused or treated a hernia. Forceful breath-holding and straining increase abdominal pressure and should be avoided.

Why does my stomach tighten during a Kegel?

A small amount of coordinated abdominal activity can occur, but strong visible bracing usually means the effort is too high or the wrong muscles are taking over. Reduce the force and keep breathing.

Should I continue if the pain is mild?

No. Stop the provoking exercise and let the symptoms settle. If pain returns when you retry gently, seek an assessment instead of training through it.

This article is for general education and is not medical advice. New, persistent, or severe abdominal or pelvic pain should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare professional.
