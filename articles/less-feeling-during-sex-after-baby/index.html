Less Feeling During Sex After a Baby? What Helps

Reviewed November 23, 2026By Sergei ProstokishynEvidence-aware guide

Less feeling during sex after a baby is common and usually improves. Stretched or weakened pelvic floor muscles, healing tears, hormone-related vaginal dryness while breastfeeding, exhaustion and low mood can all dull sensation and arousal. Pelvic floor physiotherapy, gentle Kegels once you are cleared, lubrication and time help many women.

Short answer: Reduced sensation after birth often comes from a combination of a weaker pelvic floor, healing tissue, dryness and fatigue. Get checked at your postnatal appointment, ask for pelvic floor physiotherapy, and see a doctor sooner if sex is painful or you notice bulging or heaviness.

Why You Might Feel Less During Sex After a Baby

There is rarely one cause. Most women have a mix of the following.

Pelvic floor stretch and weakness

Pregnancy and vaginal birth stretch the pelvic floor, the muscles that surround the vagina, urethra and anus. These muscles contribute to grip, arousal and the contractions of orgasm. When they are weak or hard to feel, the vagina can feel looser and sensations duller. A C-section does not fully protect the pelvic floor, because pregnancy itself loads it for months.

Perineal tears, stitches and scar tissue

Tears and episiotomy scars can leave areas that feel numb, tight or tender while nerves and tissue recover, which makes it hard to relax and enjoy sex.

Breastfeeding-related vaginal dryness

After birth, and especially while breastfeeding, estrogen levels stay low. The NHS notes that hormonal changes after birth can make the vagina drier than usual, and lists breastfeeding as a cause of vaginal dryness. Dryness causes friction and discomfort, which can numb pleasure even when the muscles are fine.

Fatigue, mood and body changes

Broken sleep, the mental load of a newborn, touch fatigue and body-image changes all reduce desire and arousal. Low arousal means less lubrication and blood flow, so sensation drops. Postnatal depression and anxiety can also flatten pleasure and deserve care in their own right.

No Sensation During Sex After Baby: Is It Normal?

Feeling "nothing" in the first weeks or months is something many new parents describe, and for most it improves as tissue heals, hormones settle and sleep returns. It is still worth raising at your postnatal check around 6–8 weeks, especially if it is not improving by several months after birth. You do not need to wait for a specific number of months to ask for help.

What Helps: A Practical Plan

  1. Get the all-clear first. Wait until bleeding has settled, stitches have healed and your midwife, doctor or postnatal check says activity is appropriate.
  2. Ask for pelvic floor physiotherapy. A pelvic health physiotherapist can check whether your muscles are weak, tight or poorly coordinated, and tailor exercises. In some countries this is routine after birth; elsewhere you may need to ask.
  3. Start gentle Kegels once cleared. Breathe out, gently close and lift around the vagina and anus, hold for a few comfortable seconds, then release fully. Build slowly. See Kegels After Childbirth for a first routine and Kegel Exercises for Women for technique.
  4. Use lubricant generously. A water-based or silicone-based lubricant reduces friction from dryness. Avoid perfumed products.
  5. Take more time for arousal. Longer foreplay and non-penetrative touch let blood flow and natural lubrication build before penetration.
  6. Protect sleep and mood where you can. Sharing night feeds, rest and support for low mood all feed back into desire and sensation.

Do Kegels Help Sensation After Birth?

They may. A 2019 systematic review of 10 studies found that most reported improvements in desire, arousal, orgasm or satisfaction with postpartum pelvic floor exercise. The authors warned that many studies were small and at risk of bias, so this is encouraging rather than conclusive. The NHS also notes that pelvic floor exercises can make sex better.

Kegels are not right for everyone, though. If sex is painful or your pelvic floor feels tight, more squeezing can make things worse; relaxation and down-training come first. Weak vs Tight Pelvic Floor explains the difference.

When to See a Doctor

Book an appointment with your GP, midwife, OB-GYN or a pelvic health physiotherapist if you have:

  • Pain during sex that does not ease, or pain at a scar.
  • A feeling of heaviness, dragging or a bulge in the vagina, which can be a sign of prolapse.
  • Leaking urine, wind or stool.
  • Numbness that is getting worse rather than better.
  • Bleeding after sex, unusual discharge or signs of infection.
  • Low mood, anxiety or loss of interest that lasts more than a couple of weeks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is there less feeling during sex after a baby?

Usually a mix of a stretched or weaker pelvic floor, healing tears or scars, low estrogen and dryness while breastfeeding, and tiredness or low mood. Most of these improve with time and the right support.

How long does loss of sensation during sex after birth last?

It varies. Many women notice gradual improvement over the first months as tissue heals and hormones settle. If there is no improvement after several months, ask for a pelvic floor assessment.

Will sensation come back after breastfeeding?

Dryness linked to low estrogen often eases when breastfeeding reduces or stops and periods return. Lubricant helps in the meantime, and a doctor can discuss other options if dryness persists.

Should I do Kegels if sex hurts after birth?

Not without assessment. Pain can mean healing problems or an over-tight pelvic floor, which needs relaxation rather than extra squeezing.

This article is for general education and is not medical advice. If sex is painful, you notice bulging or leaking, or your mood is low, see your doctor, midwife or a pelvic floor physical therapist.
