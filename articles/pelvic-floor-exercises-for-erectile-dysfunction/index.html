Pelvic Floor Exercises for Erectile Dysfunction: A Routine

Reviewed November 23, 2026By Sergei ProstokishynEvidence-aware guide

Pelvic floor exercises for erectile dysfunction usually mean strong pelvic floor squeezes done lying, sitting and standing every morning and evening, plus light lifts while walking, kept up for about three months. The routine below is modeled on the best-known randomized trial of this approach. It may help some men, but it works best after a medical check and alongside other healthy changes.

Short answer: Do 3 strong holds lying, 3 sitting and 3 standing, twice a day, add a gentle lift while walking and a squeeze after urinating, and review progress at 3 months. See a doctor first, because ED can be an early sign of heart disease.

See a Doctor Before You Start

Erectile dysfunction is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Mayo Clinic explains that ED can be an early warning sign of heart disease, because the smaller arteries in the penis may show blood flow problems before heart symptoms appear.

NIDDK lists vascular disease, diabetes, hormone problems, nerve damage, medicines and psychological factors among the causes. A clinician can check blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol and hormones. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own; ask the prescriber to review it.

What the Evidence Says, in One Paragraph

A small 2004 trial by Dorey and colleagues found that pelvic floor exercises with biofeedback and lifestyle advice improved erectile function more than lifestyle advice alone at three months. Later reviews are mixed, and one 2024 meta-analysis found clearer benefit from aerobic exercise than from pelvic floor training alone. We cover the details in Can Kegels help erectile dysfunction?. This page is the practical side.

Why Pelvic Floor Exercises May Help Erectile Function

Two muscles at the base of the penis, the ischiocavernosus and bulbocavernosus, contract during an erection. Their contraction raises pressure inside the penis, and the bulbocavernosus helps limit blood flowing back out. That is the idea behind pelvic floor exercises for erectile function. Blood vessels, nerves and hormones still do most of the work, so exercises are an add-on, not a replacement for care.

Pelvic Floor Exercises for Erectile Dysfunction: The Routine

In the Dorey trial, a physiotherapist taught each man individually over five weekly 30-minute sessions using biofeedback. At home, men did the exercises daily. The handout looked roughly like this:

That is 18 strong contractions a day. In the trial, hold length was set individually by the physiotherapist. At home, a sensible approach is to hold only as long as you can keep good form, often 3 to 5 seconds at first, and build toward about 10 seconds. Rest at least as long as you held.

The handout also suggested rhythmic pelvic floor tightening during sexual activity to help maintain rigidity. Treat that as optional practice once the basic squeeze feels easy.

Your 12-Week Plan

  1. Weeks 1–2: learn. Start lying down. Find the lift, keep breathing, relax fully between holds. Our guide on finding your pelvic floor muscles helps here.
  2. Weeks 3–6: full routine. Add sitting and standing, twice daily, 3 holds per position.
  3. Weeks 7–12: build. Lengthen the holds, add the walking lift and the post-urination squeeze.
  4. Week 12: review. In the trial, outcomes were measured at 3 months, and men kept exercising toward 6 months.

Pair It With Lifestyle Changes

Men in the trial also got advice on smoking, alcohol, general fitness, healthy diet, weight and saddle pressure from cycling. A 2024 meta-analysis found aerobic exercise improved erectile function, so brisk walking, cycling or swimming several times a week is a strong partner to the routine.

When to Stop or Change Course

Stop strengthening and get assessed if you notice pelvic pain, pain after ejaculation, a weak or hesitant urine stream, or symptoms that get worse after exercising. These can point to an overactive pelvic floor, which needs relaxation work rather than more squeezing. See reverse Kegels for men.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do pelvic floor exercises for erectile function really work?

Studies show they may help some men, especially in supervised programs with biofeedback. Evidence is mixed overall, and results are not guaranteed.

Is there a Kegel exercises for erectile dysfunction physiotherapy guide I can follow?

The routine above follows the published Dorey trial handout. A pelvic floor physical therapist can tailor hold times and check your technique, which home routines cannot do.

Do Kegels help with erectile strength?

They may help rigidity for some men because the muscles at the base of the penis support erections. Blood flow, nerves and hormones matter more, so a medical check comes first.

Can Kegel exercises cause erectile dysfunction?

There is no good evidence that normal Kegels cause ED. Overdoing them, or training a pelvic floor that is already too tight, can cause pain or discomfort. See can you do too many Kegels?.

This article is for general education and is not medical advice. For erectile problems, see your doctor, a urologist, sexual medicine clinician or pelvic floor physical therapist.
